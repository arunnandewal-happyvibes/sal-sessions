#### **Scene 3.2**

1. **A report claims a machine's failure rate "tripled" based on failures going from 2 to 6 out of 50,000 units. What is the biggest risk in repeating this claim as-is?**  
   a) There is no risk; the arithmetic is correct so the claim is safe to repeat  
   b) The tiny sample size (2 to 6) makes "tripled" sound far more dramatic than it really is  
   c) Failure rates can never be expressed as percentages  
   d) The AI must have refused to answer this question  

   **Answer:** b) The tiny sample size (2 to 6) makes "tripled" sound far more dramatic than it really is  

   **Explanation:** The maths is technically right, but with such tiny counts the word "tripled" creates a false sense of alarm — checking the sample size behind a dramatic-sounding claim is a core sanity check.

2. **Two events happened together: a website redesign launched, and sign-ups rose the same week. Which is the more careful way to describe this in a report?**  
   a) "Sign-ups rose the same week as the redesign; other causes have not yet been ruled out."  
   b) "The redesign caused the rise in sign-ups."  
   c) "The redesign had absolutely no effect."  
   d) "This proves redesigns always increase sign-ups."  

   **Answer:** a) "Sign-ups rose the same week as the redesign; other causes have not yet been ruled out."  

   **Explanation:** Two things happening at the same time is correlation, not proof of causation — a careful report says what happened together and flags that the cause has not been confirmed.

3. **Which recommendation would pass a "decision-ready" bar in a short report?**  
   a) "Do more marketing."  
   b) "Numbers went up, which is good."  
   c) "A/B test a post-signup onboarding email against the current flow for two weeks, and measure activation rate."  
   d) "Improve the user experience somehow."  

   **Answer:** c) "A/B test a post-signup onboarding email against the current flow for two weeks, and measure activation rate."  

   **Explanation:** This names a specific, testable action and states exactly how success will be measured — the two features every decision-ready recommendation needs.
